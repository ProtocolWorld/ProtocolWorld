Acid-Fast Staining

About 30 min · 2026-11-20

Ziehl–Neelsen (hot) and Kinyoun (cold) acid-fast staining of bacterial smears with carbolfuchsin, 3% acid–alcohol and methylene blue, including reagent recipes, timings, controls and safety notes.

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To detect acid-fast bacteria, such as mycobacteria, by differential staining of heat-fixed smears, and to distinguish them from non-acid-fast organisms such as E. coli.

Principle

The cell walls of mycobacteria contain waxy mycolic acids that bind the phenolic dye carbolfuchsin once it is driven in with heat (Ziehl–Neelsen) or high phenol concentration (Kinyoun). Acid-fast cells then resist decolorization with acid–alcohol and stay red. Other bacteria lose the red dye and take up the methylene blue counterstain, appearing blue [1][2]. Stain strength, times and the number of fields read strongly affect sensitivity: a proficiency-testing study found many laboratories used incorrect stain concentrations and times, and reading fewer than 300 fields lowered sensitivity [2].

Materials and reagents

ItemDetails
Ziehl–Neelsen carbolfuchsin0.3 g basic fuchsin, 10 mL 95% ethanol, 5 mL melted phenol, 95 mL distilled water; mix, stand several days, filter before use [1]
Kinyoun carbolfuchsin (cold method)Solution A: 4 g basic fuchsin in 20 mL ethanol. Solution B: 8 g melted phenol in 100 mL distilled water. Mix A and B and stand a few days [1]
Acid–alcohol3% HCl in 95% ethanol (97 mL 95% ethanol + 3 mL concentrated HCl); add the acid slowly to the ethanol [1][2]
Methylene blue counterstain0.3% (0.3 g in 100 mL distilled water) [1][2]
Glass slides and smear materialClean slides; a thin film from a young culture or specimen; use distilled or deionized water, not tap water [1]
Control culturesA nonpathogenic mycobacterium as the positive control and E. coli (or another non-acid-fast species) as the negative control
Immersion oil and bibulous paperFor 1000× examination and drying

Equipment

  • Bunsen burner or alcohol lamp, or a hot plate
  • Staining rack over a sink
  • Light microscope with 100× oil objective
  • Inoculating loop and slide warmer or heat block (optional)
  • Forceps and slide tray

Procedure

  1. Place a drop of distilled water on a clean slide and emulsify a small amount of culture into a thin, even film about 2 × 1 cm. Include the controls on separate slides [1][3]. (5 min)
  2. Air-dry the smear completely, then heat-fix by passing the slide film-side up through a flame 3–4 times [2]. Alternatively, heat at 80 °C for at least 15 min [1]. Do not overheat. (5 min)
  3. Ziehl–Neelsen (hot): cover the smear with a piece of paper and flood with carbolfuchsin. Heat gently from below until steam rises; keep stain on for 5 min without boiling [1][2][5]. (5 min)
  4. Rinse thoroughly with distilled water until the runoff is colorless. Overheating can spatter the stain or crack the slide [1]. (1 min)
  5. Decolorize with 3% acid–alcohol, adding it drop by drop until the smear is faint pink and the runoff is clear (about 1–3 min). Rinse with water [1][2][5]. (1–3 min)
  6. Counterstain with 0.3% methylene blue for 30–60 s. Longer counterstaining can mask red cells. Rinse and air-dry on a rack or between bibulous paper without rubbing [1][2][3]. (30–60 s)
  7. Kinyoun (cold) alternative: stain with Kinyoun carbolfuchsin 4–5 min at room temperature with no heat. Rinse, decolorize 2–3 min, rinse, then counterstain 30 s [1][4]. (10 min)
  8. Put a drop of immersion oil on the smear and examine at 1000× (100× oil objective). Acid-fast cells are red; everything else is blue [1][3]. (5–15 min)
  9. Scan systematically. For a diagnostic-style read, examine at least 300 fields before calling a smear negative [1][2]. Record the red-rod count per field. (10 min)
  10. Discard slides in a sharps container, collect stain and rinse waste as hazardous waste, and wipe the staining area with disinfectant [1][4].

Pause points

  • After heat-fixing (step 2), smears can be stored dry in a slide box for several days.
  • After counterstaining and drying (step 6), stained slides can be kept for later reading.

Expected results

  • Positive control: bright red rods against a blue background.
  • Negative control (E. coli): blue cells only, with no red cells.
  • Test sample: red rods indicate acid-fast organisms; blue cells are non-acid-fast [1].

Controls

  • Positive control: a nonpathogenic mycobacterium smear processed alongside the sample.
  • Negative control: an E. coli or other non-acid-fast smear on the same slide or run.
  • Reagent check: a mixed positive and negative smear confirms correct decolorization.
  • Use one new, clean slide per specimen to avoid carryover [3].

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
Everything stains blue (false negative)Smear too thick or thin, weak carbolfuchsin, insufficient heating or over-decolorizationMake a thin smear, use fresh stain at the correct strength, keep steaming for 5 min, shorten the acid–alcohol step [1][2][3]
Everything stains redUnder-decolorization or thick smearDecolorize until runoff is clear; make a thinner smear [1]
Red crystals or debris look like bacilliStain precipitate or reused slidesFilter stain; use new slides; wipe the oil lens between slides [3]
Weak red staining of known positivesOld or incorrect carbolfuchsin; Kinyoun method has lower sensitivityPrepare fresh stain; consider the hot Ziehl–Neelsen method or a fluorochrome stain [1][2][3]
Stain spatters or the slide cracksOverheating or boilingHeat only until steam rises and replenish stain [1]

Safety and waste

  • Phenol in carbolfuchsin is poisonous, corrosive and combustible: wear gloves and eye protection, and use a fume hood when melting phenol [1].
  • Collect all stain, decolorizer and phenol-contaminated paper as hazardous waste [1][4].
  • Keep alcohol, acid–alcohol and flammable solvents away from the open flame; clear the sink area before flaming.
  • Mycobacteria can survive routine heat-fixing and become airborne: handle cultures and specimens in a biosafety cabinet at the proper containment level, and avoid aerosols [1][3].
