C. elegans Embryo Imaging

Estimated time: ≈ 1 h setup + 1–4 h imaging | Protocol World

To mount and image living C. elegans embryos, from the 2-cell stage onward, by time-lapse microscopy so that cell divisions, polarity and development can be followed and quantified.

C. elegans embryos are transparent and about 50 µm long, and a gravid hermaphrodite carries many of them. Cutting an adult releases early embryos, which are moved into a thin mount that keeps them in focus and hydrated. Either a thin agar pad with light compression (consistent orientation, easy for a first try) or 20 µm polystyrene beads (no compression, random orientation) is used, and the coverslip is sealed to stop drying. Low-intensity imaging with brightfield or low laser power limits phototoxicity, and a z-stack is collected at set intervals [1][2][3].

ItemDetails
C. elegans strainN2 or a fluorescent reporter strain, grown on NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50 at 20 °C; use well-fed young gravid adults (about 5 per slide) [1][2]
M9 bufferPer liter: 3 g KH₂PO₄, 6 g Na₂HPO₄, 5 g NaCl; autoclave 20 min at 120 °C, cool, then add 1 mL sterile 1 M MgSO₄ [2]
Egg salts (alternative to M9)118 mM NaCl, 40 mM KCl, 3.4 mM MgCl₂, 3.4 mM CaCl₂, 5 mM HEPES, pH 7.4; a 0.7× dilution was used for imaging in microdevices [3]
Agar for padsAbout 5% agar, melted; 3–4 drops per pad, made right before use [1]
Polystyrene beads, 20 µmDiluted 10-fold in M9; use about 4 µL per coverslip [2]
Coverslips and slidesOne 24 × 60 mm #1.5 coverslip and one small 18 × 18 mm or 20 × 20 mm coverslip, or three glass slides for pads; single-depression slide for dissection [1][2]
SealantMelted Vaseline (petroleum jelly) or VALAP, applied with a fine brush [1][2]
Dissection toolsTwo 27-gauge needles or a #15 scalpel; eyelash glued to a toothpick or pick; worm pick [1]
Mouth pipettePulled glass capillary (tip about 40 µm), tubing, and a 0.2–0.45 µm hydrophobic filter before the mouthpiece [1][2]
  • Stereomicroscope with cool light (80× zoom helps) [1]
  • DIC or spinning-disc/confocal microscope, 40×–63× objective
  • Stage temperature control, 20–22 °C
  • Time-lapse and z-stack software
  • Pick about 5 healthy gravid adults into a drop of M9 on a depression slide or coverslip. Shake them gently so bacteria wash off [1][2]. (5 min)
  • Prepare the mount. Agar pad: put 3–4 drops of molten 5% agar on a slide between two taped slides, press a fourth slide on top, and wait for the agar to set [1]. (5 min)
  • Or bead mount: place 4 µL of 20 µm beads (1:10 in M9) in the middle of a 24 × 60 mm coverslip, so the beads act as a spacer [2]. (2 min)
  • Under the stereomicroscope, cut each adult across the body next to the vulva with a needle or scalpel so that newly fertilized embryos are released [1][2]. (5 min)
  • Select about 10 two-cell embryos (not more than 15–20, which can suffocate) with an eyelash or mouth pipette. One-cell embryos are fragile, so start at 2 cells [1]. (5 min)
  • Agar pad: move the embryos with about 20 µL M9 to the pad edge, brush them into one layer, and lower an 18 × 18 mm coverslip from one side so it lands on them before the liquid [1]. (3 min)
  • Bead mount: transfer the embryos into the bead drop with the mouth pipette, then put a smaller 18 × 18 mm or 20 × 20 mm coverslip on top without pressing [2]. (3 min)
  • Trim extra agar with a razor blade. Seal all coverslip edges with melted Vaseline or VALAP. Do not move the top coverslip once it is sealed [1][2]. (3 min)
  • Find embryos by brightfield at 5× or 10×, then switch to a 40× or 60× oil or water objective. Avoid epifluorescence for focusing so the sample is not bleached [2]. (5 min)
  • Set the stage at 20–22 °C. For a starting point, collect 30 planes at 1 µm spacing, one stack every minute. Use low laser power with longer exposure, not high power [2][3]. (5 min)
  • Run the time-lapse. For slower processes lengthen the interval to 3–10 min; for fast events such as cortical flow, shorten it to 5 s or less [2]. (1–4 h)
  • Check for saturation in the first frames and keep checking development as the run goes. After long runs, confirm that embryos keep dividing and hatch [2]. (during run)
  • Save the files, then remove the slide and put it in the biohazard container. Quantify with open-source tools such as Fiji [2]. (15 min)
  • After step 8: sealed mounts can wait at 20 °C for a few minutes before imaging, but do not leave embryos on the slide for more than about 15 min before imaging starts [1].
  • Embryos at the 2-cell stage divide asynchronously: AB first, then P1, then they proceed to 4 cells in the expected T-shape.
  • Cell divisions, pronuclear movements and (with reporters) polarity proteins are seen clearly in the stack, and by the next day the embryos hatch if the illumination was gentle [1][2].
  • Compressed agar mounts give consistent left or right orientation, and bead mounts give random orientation without compression [1][2].
  • Wild-type (N2) embryos on the same day, to check timing
  • Unimaged embryos from the same mount, to show that mounting and illumination did not slow development
  • Compare the same stage and temperature for every strain and condition [2]
  • For drug or RNAi tests, use untreated embryos in parallel [3]
ProblemLikely causeSolution
Agar pad dries outPad made too early, or heat from the stereoscope lampMake pads right before use and use a cool light source [1]
Embryos wash away to the coverslip edgeToo much M9Use about 20 µL or less and lower the coverslip from one edge [1]
Air bubbles under the coverslipToo little bufferAdd a bit more M9, but not so much that embryos move [1]
Embryos fail to develop after mountingOne-cell embryos damaged; too many embryos; heavy illuminationUse 2-cell embryos, about 10 per mount, and low laser power [1][2]
Embryos stick to the pick or needleSurface tensionPress the wire gently on the agar pad several times [1]
Embryo drift or movementLate embryos twitch and moveUse shorter experiments or bead spacers; recheck timing for slower strains [2]
  • Sharp needles, blades and slides: dispose of them in a sharps container.
  • Always use a mouth pipette with an in-line 0.2–0.45 µm hydrophobic filter, or a hand-held aspirator, so that no biological material reaches the mouth [2].
  • Class 3B and 4 lasers can damage eyes: use the microscope's interlocks and never look into the beam.
  • Treat worms, E. coli and slides as biological waste (BSL-1); autoclave or soak them in bleach. Melted agar and VALAP are hot.
