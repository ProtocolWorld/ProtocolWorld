Bradford Protein Assay

45–60 min · 2026-11-17

Estimate protein concentration in a 96-well plate or cuvette with Coomassie G-250 and a BSA standard curve, including interference limits and curve fitting.

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To estimate the protein concentration of lysates, purified fractions and other aqueous samples with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 dye binding, in a 96-well plate (main method) or in cuvettes, using a BSA standard curve.

Principle

In acid, free Coomassie G-250 is brownish-red (absorbance maximum about 465 nm). When it binds protein, mainly through arginine, lysine and histidine side chains and hydrophobic contacts, the dye turns blue and absorbs at 595 nm; the increase in A595 reflects protein mass [1][3]. Because the response depends on amino acid composition, a standard protein gives relative, not absolute, values: BSA gives roughly twice the color per mass of many other proteins, so IgG is a better calibrator for antibody samples [2][5]. Standards and blanks should be made in the same buffer as the samples [2][5].

Materials and reagents

ItemDetails
Bradford reagent (ready-to-use, 1×)Commercial reagent (for example, Merck 1.10306 or a Bio-Rad concentrate diluted to 1×), room temperature before use [2][4]
Homemade reagent (alternative)Dissolve 100 mg Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 in 50 mL 95% ethanol, add 100 mL 85% (w/v) phosphoric acid, make up to 1 L with water, filter (Whatman No. 1), store in a dark bottle at 4 °C [1][3]. A lower-dye variant uses 50 mg dye in 50 mL methanol [3]
BSA standard stock1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin in water or in the sample buffer; for antibody samples use bovine IgG instead [2][4][5]
Sample buffer (blank)The same lysis or storage buffer as the unknowns, protein-free [2][5]
WaterDeionized or distilled, for dilutions and the standard blank
LabwareClear flat-bottom 96-well plate; microcentrifuge tubes; disposable plastic cuvettes (polystyrene or PMMA) for the cuvette method; never quartz, because the dye adsorbs to it [2]

Equipment

  • Microplate reader with a 595 nm filter or monochromator
  • Spectrophotometer (595 nm) for the cuvette format
  • Multichannel pipette and 2–20 µL and 20–200 µL pipettes
  • Vortex mixer and microcentrifuge
  • Spreadsheet or curve-fitting software

Procedure

  1. Prepare the 1 mg/mL BSA stock and warm the Bradford reagent to room temperature. Prepare blanks and standards in the same buffer as your samples [2][5]. (10 min)
  2. Make 500 µL standards at 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mg/mL: for example, 25, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µL of stock, made up to 500 µL with buffer [4]. (10 min)
  3. Clear turbid samples by centrifuging (for example 10 min at 10,000 × g) and use the supernatant [2][5]. Keep lysates on ice until pipetting. (10 min)
  4. Dilute each unknown so that it falls inside the standard range; prepare two or three dilutions (for example 1:5, 1:10 and 1:20) in sample buffer [4].
  5. Pipette 300 µL of 1× Bradford reagent into each well first, then add 10 µL of standard or diluted sample. Use triplicate wells for every standard and unknown [4]. (10 min)
  6. Mix gently on a plate shaker or by tapping for 30 s; avoid bubbles, which scatter light. Incubate 5 min at room temperature, protected from direct light [3][5]. (5 min)
  7. Read absorbance at 595 nm within 30 min; the color keeps rising slowly and is stable for about 1 h, so read all plates at the same time after mixing [1][3]. (5 min)
  8. Cuvette alternative (0.1–1.4 mg/mL): mix 50 µL sample or standard with 2.5 mL reagent in a plastic cuvette, wait 2–5 min, read at 595 nm against the reagent blank [2][3].
  9. Microassay alternative (1–10 µg protein in up to 0.1 mL): add 1 mL reagent, mix, and read at 595 nm in 1 mL cuvettes [1].
  10. Average the replicates and subtract the 0 mg/mL blank. Plot A595 (y) against concentration (x) [2][4]. (5 min)
  11. Fit a linear regression to the linear part of the curve; drop the top one or two points if the curve flattens. Use a quadratic fit if it bends smoothly [2][4].
  12. Aim for R² above 0.95. Calculate each unknown from the fit, multiply by its dilution factor, and average the dilutions that fall inside the standard range [4][5].

Pause points

  • Standards can be stored frozen in aliquots for later curves, but prepare a fresh curve for each assay when possible [4][5].
  • The homemade reagent keeps for several months in a dark bottle at 4 °C; filter again if it precipitates [3][5].
  • Once reagent is added, read within 1 h; do not pause the reaction beyond that [1][3].

Expected results

  • The blank wells are brown-red and the standards turn progressively bluer; A595 rises with concentration, roughly linearly over the lower part of the range [1][2].
  • A good standard curve gives R² of 0.95 or higher (r of 0.9–1.0 is typical); keep absorbance below about 1.2–1.5 on a plate reader, where the curve flattens [4][5].
  • Report concentration as mg/mL of the original sample, relative to the standard protein used [2].

Controls

  • 0 mg/mL blank made in the sample buffer, subtracted from every well [2][4]
  • Standard curve run in triplicate on every plate
  • A buffer-only control with any detergent present, to check interference [2]
  • An independent sample of known concentration, if available, to check accuracy

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
Curve flattens at the topDye becomes limiting or the response leaves the linear rangeDrop the top standards, dilute samples, or use the lower-range micro format [2][4]
High blank or strange colorDetergent, reducing agent or other interferent in the bufferMatch the blank to the sample buffer; keep SDS, Triton X-100 and deoxycholate at or below 0.1% and Tween 20 below 0.5%, or use a detergent-compatible assay [2][5]
Replicates disagreeSmall pipetting volumes, bubbles, uneven mixingPipette 10 µL carefully, mix gently, use triplicates and a multichannel pipette [2]
Result differs from another assayDifferent protein–dye response between the standard and your sampleUse IgG for antibodies; compare only with the same standard protein [2][5]
Turbid wellsInsoluble material or precipitated dyeCentrifuge or filter samples; filter the reagent; read against a matched blank [2][5]
Stained cuvettes or plateDye adsorbs to surfacesUse disposable plastic, never quartz; rinse stained plates with methanol, then water [2][4]

Safety and waste

  • The reagent contains phosphoric acid and methanol or ethanol: wear gloves, safety glasses and a lab coat, and rinse with water after skin or eye contact [2][5].
  • Keep reagent away from open flames and heat sources because of the alcohol content [5].
  • Dispose of used reagent and dye-stained plates as chemical waste according to your institution's rules; do not pour concentrated reagent down the drain.
  • Treat lysates from human or other potentially infectious material at the biosafety level your institution requires.
