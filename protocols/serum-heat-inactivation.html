Serum Heat Inactivation

2–3 h (plus overnight thaw) · 2026-11-26

Heat-inactivate fetal bovine serum at 56 °C for 30 minutes with a control bottle, correct timing, mixing, rapid cooling and aliquoting. Explains when it is, and is not, needed.

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To heat-inactivate fetal bovine serum (FBS) or other animal serum so that complement is destroyed, while keeping its growth-promoting properties as intact as possible.

Principle

Serum contains heat-labile complement proteins that can lyse cells or activate immune cells in complement-sensitive work. Holding serum at 56 °C for 30 minutes denatures complement [1][3][5]. The same heat can damage growth factors and cause precipitates or gelling, so the temperature, time and mixing must be tightly controlled, and many routine cultures do not need heat inactivation [2][3]. Time is counted from the moment the serum itself reaches 56 °C, judged from a control bottle of water [2].

Materials and reagents

ItemDetails
Serum500 mL bottle of FBS or other serum, thawed and mixed; not already heat-inactivated [5]
Control bottleSame type of bottle filled with water to the same volume as the serum, stored with the serum so starting temperature matches [2]
Sterile bottles or tubesFor aliquots, for example 50 mL conical tubes or 100 mL bottles
Ice and waterIce bath for rapid cooling [1][2][3]
70% ethanolTo wipe bottles before opening
LabelsDate, lot number and 'HI' (heat-inactivated)

Equipment

  • Water bath set to 56 °C with enough water to cover the bottle up to the serum level but not the cap
  • Calibrated thermometer or thermocouple that can sit in the control bottle
  • Timer
  • Biosafety cabinet
  • −20 °C freezer
  • Weights or racks to stop bottles from floating, if needed [2]

Procedure

  1. Decide that heat inactivation is needed (for example complement-sensitive, immunological, stem cell or insect cell work); if not, skip it [2][5]. (—)
  2. Thaw the serum overnight at 4 °C, or at room temperature, or briefly in a 37 °C bath, removing it as soon as it thaws [1][4]. (overnight)
  3. Swirl the bottle gently until the serum is completely homogeneous; never heat a partly thawed or unmixed bottle [1]. (2 min)
  4. Set the water bath to 56 °C and let it stabilize. Place a thermometer in the control bottle in the same position as the serum bottle [1][2]. (30 min)
  5. Lower the serum and control bottles into the bath so the water is above the serum level but below the cap; add weights if they float [2][3]. (2 min)
  6. Start timing when the control bottle reads 56 °C, not when the bottle goes into the bath; a 500 mL plastic bottle can take 40 minutes to reach temperature [2]. (variable)
  7. Swirl both bottles gently every 5–10 minutes (5 minutes is safest) to avoid gelling and heat unevenly [1][2][3]. (during hold)
  8. Hold at 56 °C for exactly 30 minutes (±2 °C); do not exceed 30 minutes or 56 °C [1][2][3]. (30 min)
  9. Remove the bottles and cool immediately in an ice bath; serum can reach about 10 °C in 30 minutes on ice, versus hours in a refrigerator [2][3]. (30 min)
  10. Wipe the bottle with 70% ethanol, and in a biosafety cabinet divide the serum into sterile aliquots (for example 50 mL), avoiding sediment at the bottom [1][4]. (20 min)
  11. Label with date, lot and HI, then freeze at −20 °C; avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles [1][4]. (5 min)

Pause points

  • Serum can be paused after thawing overnight at 4 °C.
  • After cooling, aliquots can be frozen at −20 °C indefinitely within normal serum shelf life; do not leave warm serum standing.

Expected results

  • Clear to slightly hazy, straw-colored serum with little or no gel; light flocculent material can be normal [3][5].
  • Complement activity eliminated; cell growth in complete medium should match the previous lot in a side-by-side test.

Controls

  • Control water bottle with thermometer to confirm the 30-minute timing
  • Side-by-side growth or attachment test of heat-inactivated versus untreated serum on your cell line
  • Record bath temperature and time for each batch

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
White globules or cloudy precipitateProtein and lipid denaturation; heating too fast or too hotMix well, stay at 56 °C ± 2 °C, never exceed 30 minutes, swirl often [1][2]
Serum gels or thickensOverheating or no mixingDiscard; repeat with a new bottle and tighter control [5]
Precipitate looks like bacteriaFibrin and calcium complexes; incubation at 37 °C worsens itDo not 'culture' it at 37 °C; check by microscopy or plating if in doubt [2][3]
Cells grow worse than beforeLoss of growth factorsTest non-inactivated serum or a shorter hold; many lines do not need heat inactivation [2]
Bottle floats or heats unevenlyNot submerged enoughUse weights and cover water to the serum level [2]

Safety and waste

  • Wear gloves, a lab coat and eye protection; hot water and bottles can scald.
  • Do not submerge bottle caps in water, to avoid contamination.
  • Treat animal serum as potentially infectious; discard spills and used containers as biological waste per your institution's rules.
