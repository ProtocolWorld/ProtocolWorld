Trypan Blue Viability Assay

30–45 min · 2026-11-21

A quick dye-exclusion method to count mammalian cells and estimate the percentage that are alive using 0.4% trypan blue and a hemocytometer. Includes harvesting, 1:1 staining, grid counting and a worked calculation.

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To estimate the concentration of live cells and the percentage viability of a mammalian cell suspension by manual trypan blue dye exclusion counting.

Principle

Trypan blue is a vital dye that enters only cells whose membranes are compromised. Live cells stay unstained and bright, while dead cells turn blue [1][2]. Mixing the suspension 1:1 with 0.4% dye and counting unstained and blue cells in a hemocytometer gives viability and cell concentration [2][4]. Each large corner square holds 0.1 mm³ (10⁻⁴ mL), which gives the 10⁴ factor [2][3]. Serum proteins also bind the dye, so cells should be counted in serum-free buffer or medium [5]. Prolonged dye exposure kills cells, so counting must be prompt [2][4][5].

Equipment

  • Inverted or upright light microscope with 10× objective
  • Biosafety cabinet
  • Centrifuge (about 100–300 × g)
  • Micropipettes (P20, P200)
  • Timer
  • Tally counter

Procedure

  1. Harvest cells. For adherent cultures, rinse with DPBS, add trypsin-EDTA, detach, neutralize and pipette to a single-cell suspension [2][4]. (10–15 min)
  2. For suspension cultures, swirl the flask and sample at once before cells settle; resuspend fully by pipetting [3][4]. (2 min)
  3. If the medium contains serum, pellet cells (about 100 × g, 5 min), discard the supernatant and resuspend in 1 mL PBS or serum-free medium [5]. (7 min)
  4. Adjust density to about 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL; dilute or concentrate by centrifugation if it is far off [2]. Note any dilution factor (DF) used. (3 min)
  5. Clean the hemocytometer and coverslip with 70% ethanol, dry, and seat the coverslip firmly over the chamber [2][3]. (2 min)
  6. Mix 10 µL of well-suspended cells with 10 µL of 0.4% trypan blue (1:1, dye factor 2) in a tube; pipette gently [1][2]. (1 min)
  7. Pause point: start a timer. Count within about 3–5 min and never leave cells in the dye longer than 15 min [2][3][5]. (3–5 min)
  8. Load 10 µL of the mixture at the chamber edge and let capillary action fill it. Do not overfill or move the coverslip [1][2][3]. (1 min)
  9. Rest 1–2 min so cells settle, then focus on the grid at 10× [2][5]. (1–2 min)
  10. Count unstained (viable) and blue (dead) cells in the four corner squares; aim for about 20–100 cells per square [2][3][5]. (5 min)
  11. Use one border rule: count cells on the top and left lines but not the bottom and right (or the reverse) [2][3]. (—)
  12. If counts exceed about 100 per square, remake a more dilute sample and recount; if very low, concentrate or count more squares [3][5]. (5 min)
  13. Calculate cells/mL = mean viable count per square × 10⁴ × 2 × sample DF [2]. (3 min)
  14. Calculate viability (%) = viable cells ÷ (viable + blue cells) × 100. Healthy log-phase cultures usually reach at least 95% [1][2]. (2 min)
  15. Clean the chamber with ethanol and dispose of the dye mixture as hazardous waste. Record counts, dilution and viability [2]. (5 min)

Pause points

  • The protocol can be paused after sterilization and labeling; store solutions as described.

Expected results

  • Live cells appear round, clear and unstained; dead cells appear blue and often swollen [2].
  • Healthy log-phase cultures typically show 95% viability or higher [1].
  • Worked example (four corner squares, 1:1 dye, no prior dilution): viable counts 42, 51, 47 and 55 (total 195, mean 48.75); blue counts 5, 3, 6 and 4 (total 18).
  • Viable cells/mL = 48.75 × 10⁴ × 2 = 9.75 × 10⁵ (about 1.0 × 10⁶). Total cells/mL = (213 ÷ 4) × 10⁴ × 2 = 1.07 × 10⁶.
  • Viability = 195 ÷ (195 + 18) × 100 = 91.5%. A 1:10 prestain dilution would multiply the concentrations by 10 [2].
  • The method does not distinguish apoptotic from necrotic cells, and it can overestimate viability in aging cultures compared with fluorescent assays [2][6]. Automated image-based counters (for example Vi-CELL or Countess) use the same principle and can be validated against manual counts [2].

Controls

  • Count at least two chambers or two independent samples and compare the results
  • Include a heat-killed or freeze-thawed aliquot (mostly blue cells) to confirm that the dye works
  • Use the same counting rule and the same timing for all samples
  • Record the exact time between mixing and counting for every sample

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
Viability appears falsely low, with blue background and many stained cellsSerum proteins bind the dye, or cells sat in dye too longResuspend in PBS or serum-free medium and count within 3–5 min [2][5]
Counts differ widely between squaresPoor mixing, cells clumped or settled, uneven loadingResuspend thoroughly, break clumps, reload a fresh chamber [2][3]
Too many cells per square to countSuspension too denseDilute and recount; aim for about 20–100 cells per square and note the DF [3][5]
Too few cells for reliable countsSuspension too dilute (below about 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL)Concentrate by gentle centrifugation or count more squares [2]
Dye crystals or debris confused with cellsOld or unfiltered trypan blueFilter the stain through 0.2 µm and use fresh aliquots [2][5]
Viability seems too high for a stressed or aging cultureDye exclusion misses early dying cells with intact membranesConfirm with a fluorescent live/dead assay or an automated counter [2][6]
Chamber fills unevenly or has air bubblesDirty chamber or coverslip not seatedClean with ethanol, reseat the coverslip and reload [2][3]

Safety and waste

  • Trypan blue is a suspected carcinogen: wear a lab coat, gloves and eye protection, and avoid skin contact and dust.
  • Collect used trypan blue, stained cells and contaminated tips as chemical waste per institutional rules; do not pour them down the drain.
  • Treat all cells as potentially infectious; work in a biosafety cabinet and decontaminate hemocytometers with 70% ethanol after use.
  • Dispose of coverslips and sharps in the proper containers; follow your institution's rules for biohazard waste.
