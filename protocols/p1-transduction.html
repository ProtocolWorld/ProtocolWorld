P1 Transduction

4–5 days (lysate 1 day, transduction 2–3 days) · 2026-11-27

Prepare and titer a P1vir lysate on a donor E. coli K-12 strain, then transduce a selectable marker into a recipient: Ca²⁺ adsorption, citrate stop, expression, selection and purification.

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To move a selectable chromosomal marker (for example a Keio kan cassette) from a donor E. coli K-12 strain into a recipient strain using bacteriophage P1vir, and to prepare and titer the high-titer P1 lysate needed.

Principle

Phage P1 occasionally packages about 90–100 kb of host DNA instead of its own genome, so roughly 1 in 10³ particles in a lysate carries donor chromosomal DNA. Infection of a recipient delivers that fragment, and RecA-dependent recombination can integrate it. Calcium is required for phage adsorption, and sodium citrate removes free Ca²⁺ afterwards so that no further infection occurs [1][2]. Transductants are selected on antibiotic plates and purified by re-streaking to remove carried-over phage [1][3].

Equipment

  • 37 °C shaking incubator (30–32 °C for temperature-sensitive strains)
  • Fume hood
  • Refrigerated centrifuge for 35 mL tubes (about 9,200 × g)
  • Benchtop microcentrifuge
  • 50 mL flasks or 18 × 150 mm tubes
  • 50 °C water bath or heat block for top agar
  • Sterile glass or screw-cap storage tubes
  • Spreaders and sterile pipette tips

Procedure

  1. Streak donor and recipient and grow 5 mL LB overnight cultures at 37 °C; use 30–32 °C for temperature-sensitive strains [1]. (overnight)
  2. Dilute the donor overnight culture 1:100 into 5 mL LB with 50 µL 20% glucose and 25 µL 1 M CaCl₂ in a 50 mL flask [1]. (5 min)
  3. Shake at 30–37 °C until early log phase (OD₆₀₀ about 0.5, not above 0.6) [1][3]. (30–45 min)
  4. Add 100 µL P1vir at 10⁹–10¹⁰ pfu/mL; if lysis fails, test 5–100 µL because too much phage can also fail [1][4]. (2 min)
  5. Keep shaking until the culture clears and debris appears [1][4]. (about 3 h)
  6. In a fume hood add a few drops of chloroform and shake for a few minutes to kill surviving cells [1]. (5 min)
  7. Transfer to a 35 mL tube and centrifuge at about 9,200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C [1]. (10 min)
  8. Pour the supernatant into a screw-cap tube, avoiding the pellet. Store at 4 °C and do not freeze [1][2]. (2 min)
  9. Titer: grow a K-12 strain to about 2 × 10⁸ cells/mL, add CaCl₂ to 5 mM, and make 10-fold dilutions of lysate in TM buffer down to 10⁻⁸ [1]. (1 h)
  10. Mix 0.25 mL cells with 2.5 mL top agar, pour on an LB plate, spot 10 µL of each dilution, dry, and incubate at 37 °C overnight [1]. (overnight)
  11. Count plaques: pfu/mL = plaques × dilution factor × 100 (for 10 µL spots). If below 10⁹ pfu/mL, make a new lysate [1]. (10 min)
  12. Recipient: grow a 5 mL LB overnight culture at 37 °C [1]. (overnight)
  13. Spin 1.5 mL of recipient culture for 2 min at room temperature and resuspend in 0.75 mL P1 salts, enough for two transductions [1]. (10 min)
  14. Set up tubes with 100 µL cells plus 1, 10 or 100 µL lysate; include a cells-only tube, and spread 100 µL lysate alone on a plate as phage control [1]. (10 min)
  15. Adsorb for 30 min at room temperature or at the culture temperature without shaking [1]. (30 min)
  16. Add 1 mL LB and 200 µL 1 M sodium citrate to every tube to stop infection [1]. (2 min)
  17. Incubate about 1 h at 30–37 °C with aeration so the resistance marker is expressed [1][4]. (1 h)
  18. Spin 2 min at maximum speed, discard the supernatant, resuspend in 50–100 µL LB, and spread all of it on selective plates (5 mM citrate helps) [1]. (10 min)
  19. Incubate plates at 37 °C overnight; allow up to 48 h or 30 °C for slow or temperature-sensitive markers [1][5]. (1–2 days)
  20. Purify: pick colonies from the plate that received the least lysate and streak on selective citrate plates; repeat once to lose phage [1][3]. (overnight)
  21. Confirm the new genotype by colony PCR or phenotype. Plaques around a streak mean P1vir is still present and the clone needs another streak [3]. (1 day)

Pause points

  • Finished lysate can be stored at 4 °C for months and reused for many transductions (step 8).
  • Purified transductants can be kept as a glycerol stock after the second streak.

Expected results

  • A clear, debris-containing lysate with a titer of about 10⁹–10¹⁰ pfu/mL [1].
  • About 100–1,000 transductants per mL of high-titer lysate per marker; plates from a good lysate often show 10–2,000 colonies [1][4].
  • No colonies on the cells-only and phage-only plates.
  • Selected marker present in re-streaked colonies, and no plaques when re-streaked.

Controls

  • Cells-only (recipient without phage) to check spontaneous resistance
  • Lysate-only plate to confirm the lysate has no donor cells
  • Titer spot of a known sensitive strain
  • Donor DNA marker verified by PCR in the final strain

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
Lysate titer below 10⁹ pfu/mLToo little or too much phage, low Ca²⁺, culture past early logRe-test phage volume, confirm 5 mM CaCl₂, or make a plate lysate (titers 10–100× higher) [1][3]
No transductantsrecA recipient, low titer, no Ca²⁺ at adsorption, marker too far from the selected geneConfirm recA⁺ and use fresh lysate; check CaCl₂; a recA mutation should be introduced last [1]
Cells-only plate has coloniesAntibiotic too low or plate too oldRemake selective plates and check antibiotic concentration
Many tiny colonies with phage-like clearingCarried-over P1virRestreak on citrate plates; keep citrate in the plate [1][3]
Poor adsorption in B strains or galU mutantsRestriction or defective LPSUse K-12 recipients or another method; galU cannot support P1 [1][4]

Safety and waste

  • Chloroform is volatile, toxic and a suspected carcinogen: use it only in a fume hood with gloves and eye protection [1][2].
  • P1vir is a BSL-1 bacteriophage but will contaminate shared equipment; use dedicated pipettes and decontaminate surfaces with bleach.
  • Treat phage lysates and plates as biohazardous waste: autoclave, or soak in fresh bleach, before disposal. Collect chloroform waste separately.
