Fluorescence Polarization Assay

Estimated time: Day 1: 3 h set-up and tracer tests; Day 2: 3–5 h binding or competition run | Protocol World

To measure the binding of a small fluorescent ligand (tracer) to a larger protein in solution, determine an apparent dissociation constant (Kd), and rank unlabeled competitors by their IC50 in a homogeneous, mix-and-read format.

A fluorophore excited with polarized light emits light that stays polarized if the molecule tumbles slowly during its fluorescence lifetime, and becomes depolarized if it tumbles fast. A small free tracer tumbles quickly and gives low polarization; when bound to a large protein it tumbles slowly and gives high polarization. Polarization is calculated from parallel and perpendicular emission and reported in millipolarization units (mP): mP = 1000 × (I∥ − G × I⊥) / (I∥ + G × I⊥), where G is the instrument correction factor [1][2]. Ideally the tracer is a peptide or small molecule below about 10 kDa and the binder is at least 10-fold larger, and the tracer fluorophore has a lifetime of a few nanoseconds (fluorescein, TAMRA, Cy3B and similar) [2][3].

ItemDetails
Fluorescent tracerLabeled peptide or ligand, usually >90% labeled and purified; working range 0.1–100 nM and not above about twice its Kd (start at 1× Kd if known) [1][2][3]
Purified binding proteinAs pure as possible; filter or centrifuge to remove aggregates that scatter light [2]
Assay buffer (example)20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.01% Triton X-100, plus 0.1 mg/mL bovine gamma globulin (BGG); adapt salts to your protein [1]
DetergentAbout 0.01% of Triton X-100, NP-40, Brij-35 or Tween 20 reduces sticking and aggregation [1][3]
Carrier proteinBGG at 0.1 mg/mL; avoid BSA because it can bind the tracer [1][2]
Reducing agent1–2 mM DTT if the protein needs it [1]
Free fluorophoreFor example 1 nM fluorescein in buffer, to calibrate the G factor to 27 mP [2][3]
Competitor compounds1 mM stocks in DMSO; keep a constant final DMSO percentage in every well, and test DMSO tolerance first [1][2]
MicroplatesBlack, non-binding 384-well plates (low-binding versions reduce tracer loss to polystyrene) [1][2]
  • Plate reader with fluorescence polarization optics and filters matched to the fluorophore (for example, ex 485 / em 535 nm for fluorescein; ex 530–540 / em 570–590 nm for TAMRA)
  • Multichannel pipette or liquid handler
  • Plate centrifuge
  • Plate sealer or lid
  • Data analysis software for non-linear regression (such as GraphPad Prism)
  • Pick the fluorophore (fluorescein, TAMRA, Cy3B or similar, with a lifetime under about 6 ns) and prepare tracer stock in assay buffer or DMSO [1][2]. (30 min)
  • Calibrate: read buffer-only wells and 1 nM free fluorescein; subtract buffer signal from both channels and set G so fluorescein reads 27 mP [2][3]. (20 min)
  • Tracer test: dilute tracer from 100 nM to 0.1 nM in assay buffer with at least 4 replicates; choose a concentration giving a signal at least 3× the buffer blank and SD under 10 mP [2]. (30 min)
  • Direct binding: make 2× protein serial dilutions (eleven 2-fold steps, highest concentration well above the expected Kd) in assay buffer; leave one buffer-only column [1]. (30 min)
  • Add 12.5 µL 2× protein (or buffer) to each well of a black 384-well plate, then 12.5 µL 2× tracer to give 25 µL and the chosen final tracer concentration (for example 0.1–1 nM) [1]. (20 min)
  • Spin the plate 1 min at about 1,000 rpm, cover, and incubate at room temperature in the dark [1]. (30 min–4.5 h)
  • Read at several times (for example 30 min, 1 h, 4 h, 24 h) to find when the signal stops changing, which indicates equilibrium [1]. (Variable)
  • Competition: with tracer fixed at about 1× Kd and protein at a concentration giving about 75% tracer bound, add 2× compound in eleven 2-fold dilutions (test compounds from 10–50 µM) [1][2]. (45 min)
  • Add controls to each plate: free tracer, tracer + protein with no competitor (maximum), buffer-only blank and protein-only blank [2][3]. (15 min)
  • Incubate until equilibrium (confirm that IC50 is unchanged between two read times), then read parallel and perpendicular intensities and total fluorescence [1]. (1–4.5 h)
  • Subtract blanks, calculate mP with the G factor, and check the window (≥70–100 mP) and SD (<10 mP); for screening, calculate Z′ = 1 − 3(σ₊ + σ₋)/|μ₊ − μ₋|, aiming for >0.5 [2][3]. (30 min)
  • Fit binding data to a one-site binding model (mP versus protein concentration) for Kd, and competition data to a four- or three-parameter log(inhibitor) response for IC50 [1][2]. (30 min)
  • Dilution plates can be prepared and covered before adding tracer.
  • Plates can be read several times; incubate in the dark between reads.
  • Free tracer reads low (typically 20–50 mP); fully bound tracer is higher, with a total window of at least 70–100 mP [1][2].
  • The binding curve is hyperbolic when plotted on a linear axis (sigmoidal on a log axis), giving Kd as the protein concentration at half-maximal change.
  • The competition curve falls from the bound to the free-tracer mP value with increasing competitor; a well-behaved assay has a Hill slope near 1.
  • Maximum theoretical polarization is 500 mP; values above that indicate an artifact [2].
  • Buffer-only wells for background subtraction
  • Free tracer (no protein) as the low-mP reference
  • Tracer + saturating protein as the high-mP reference
  • Unlabeled version of the tracer as a reference competitor
  • DMSO-only wells matched to the compound wells
  • Protein-only wells to check protein autofluorescence or scattering

  • DMSO carries dissolved compounds through skin: wear gloves and a lab coat.
  • Fluorescent dyes and test compounds may be toxic; avoid skin contact and handle stock powders in a hood.
  • Dispose of dye-containing and compound-containing liquids as chemical waste.
  • If the protein comes from human cells or pathogens, follow the relevant biosafety level.
