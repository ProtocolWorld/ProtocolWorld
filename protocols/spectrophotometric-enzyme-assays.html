Spectrophotometric Enzyme Assays

About 2 hours · 2026-11-18

Continuous cuvette assay following NADH at 340 nm, with linear-rate selection, controls and U/mL and specific-activity calculations (LDH example).

⬇ Word (.docx)⬇ PowerPoint (.pptx)

Purpose

To measure the activity of an enzyme continuously in a cuvette by following the change in absorbance of a substrate or cofactor, and to convert the rate into units per milliliter and specific activity. The worked example is lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which is followed by the loss of NADH at 340 nm.

Principle

NADH absorbs light at 340 nm, whereas NAD⁺ does not. When LDH reduces pyruvate to lactate, NADH is oxidized and A₃₄₀ falls in proportion to the amount of reaction. The Beer–Lambert law, A = ε × l × c, with ε = 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for NADH and a 1 cm light path, converts the slope of the linear part of the trace into a rate in µmol per minute [1][3][4]. One unit is defined as the amount of enzyme that converts 1 µmol of substrate per minute under stated conditions, so the temperature, pH and buffer must always be reported with the result [1][2][3].

Materials and reagents

ItemDetails
Assay buffer100 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.5 at 37 °C (Sigma method) [1]. Other validated conditions include 0.2 M Tris-HCl pH 7.3 at 25 °C [2] and 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.1 at 30 °C [4]
NADH solution0.13 mM in assay buffer (about 0.10 mg/mL), prepared fresh; the powder should be white to light yellow, and discard it if yellow or clumpy [1]
Pyruvate solution34 mM in assay buffer (about 3.7 mg/mL), prepared fresh [1]
Enzyme diluentCold 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in water [1]
LDH sampleDiluted in cold 1% BSA immediately before use to 0.15–0.50 U/mL [1]
Cuvettes1 cm path length, quartz or UV-transparent; plastic cuvettes gave lower activities in one protocol [4]
Protein standard reagentsFor the specific activity, a protein assay such as Bradford or BCA

Equipment

  • UV-visible spectrophotometer reading at 340 nm with a thermostatted cuvette holder (37 °C in the example)
  • Water bath or circulator for temperature control
  • Micropipettes (10, 100, 1000 µL)
  • Ice bucket
  • Timer or spectrophotometer kinetics software

Procedure

  1. Warm the spectrophotometer and cuvette holder to the assay temperature (37 °C for the Sigma method) and set the wavelength to 340 nm [1]. (15 min)
  2. Prepare the NADH and pyruvate solutions fresh in assay buffer and keep them on ice. Do not premix substrates with each other until the cuvette is set up [1][3][4]. (15 min)
  3. Dilute the enzyme in cold 1% BSA to 0.15–0.50 U/mL just before use and keep it on ice. Record the dilution factor [1]. (10 min)
  4. For each Test and Blank, pipette 2.80 mL NADH solution and 0.10 mL pyruvate solution into a cuvette [1]. (5 min)
  5. Equilibrate the cuvettes at 37 °C until A₃₄₀ is constant, which sets the baseline drift [1][2]. (5 min)
  6. Add 0.10 mL diluted enzyme to the Test cuvette and 0.10 mL 1% BSA to the Blank cuvette. Mix quickly by inversion [1]. (1 min)
  7. Record A₃₄₀ for about 5 min at 5–10 s intervals, then identify the maximum linear section of the trace [1][4]. (5 min)
  8. Check that the slope is within the linear range of the assay (about 0.02–0.15 ΔA/min in these methods). If it is faster, dilute the enzyme and repeat [2][3][4]. (5 min)
  9. Calculate the rate as ΔA₃₄₀/min for the Test minus the Blank, and convert it to U/mL with the equation in the expected results [1]. (5 min)
  10. Measure the protein concentration of the enzyme stock and divide U/mL by mg/mL to obtain the specific activity (U/mg) [1][3]. (30 min)
  11. Repeat in triplicate. To confirm initial-rate conditions, run a series of enzyme concentrations and check that the rate is proportional to enzyme amount [3]. (30 min)

Pause points

  • Enzyme and substrate solutions are prepared fresh. Only the concentrated enzyme stock (on ice) and the protein measurement can wait.
  • Once the enzyme is added to the cuvette, record immediately; do not pause during the reaction.

Expected results

  • A340 falls linearly for the first 1–5 min in the Test cuvette, while the Blank shows little or no drift.
  • Activity in U/mL = (ΔA/min Test − ΔA/min Blank) × 3 (mL assay volume) × dilution factor / (6.22 × 0.1 mL enzyme volume) [1].
  • Worked example (illustrative arithmetic): a commercial LDH stock of 1 mg/mL is diluted 1:3,000. The Test slope is 0.066 and the Blank slope is 0.002 ΔA/min. Activity = (0.064 × 3 × 3000) / (6.22 × 0.1) = 926 U/mL, or about 926 U/mg at 1 mg/mL protein. Commercial LDH preparations are of this order (950 U/mg at 37 °C is quoted for one product) [4].
  • The same calculation can be written as v = ΔA/min × V(cuvette) / (ε × l × V(sample)) [4].

Controls

  • Blank without enzyme (BSA diluent in place of enzyme) to measure non-enzymatic NADH loss and baseline drift [1][3]
  • Reaction without substrate (no pyruvate) to detect other NADH-oxidizing activities in crude samples (general practice)
  • A commercial enzyme standard run on each day of measurement to check the assay and instrument [4]
  • A positive control of known activity, and a no-NADH control, as used in teaching protocols [3]

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeSolution
Trace is not linearToo much enzyme, so substrate is depleted or the cuvette is mixed lateDilute the enzyme so ΔA stays within the linear range, and use only the initial slope [2][3][4]
High baseline driftOld or yellow NADH, contaminated buffer, or temperature not equilibratedMake NADH fresh, check the powder, and equilibrate cuvettes until A340 is stable [1][2]
Rate is not proportional to enzyme amountSubstrate depletion, product inhibition or substrate inhibitionRun an enzyme dilution series and work in the proportional region; keep substrate below inhibitory concentrations [3]
Results vary from day to dayTemperature changes; enzyme not kept on ice; different buffer lotsUse a thermostatted holder (a 10 °C change can alter the rate about two-fold), keep enzyme on ice and run a standard each day [3][4]
Low activity in plastic cuvettesPlastic cuvettes gave lower activities in the source; the cause is not statedUse quartz or UV-transparent cuvettes [4]

Safety and waste

  • Wear gloves, a lab coat and eye protection; sodium phosphate and Tris buffers are low hazard.
  • Quartz cuvettes are fragile and can break into sharp pieces; handle carefully.
  • Treat tissue homogenates as potentially infectious and follow your institution's biosafety rules.
  • Dispose of used solutions according to local chemical waste rules; do not pour solutions with biological material down the drain without disinfection.
